The core questions are whether the model can be validly tested with such a small dataset—fewer than 10 cases over 2 years—and what timing mechanism should replace the standard NCL approach.

The key insight they've uncovered is that NCLT admission is fundamentally a legal event, not an economic one. In India, there's a massive lag between when a company actually defaults and when it gets admitted to the insolvency process—median of around 1-2 years based on IBBI data, with Section 7 and 9 petitions taking far longer than the statutory 14 days. This heterogeneity in what "admission" actually represents is a major problem for using it as a clock. Better anchors would be the first payment default date itself, or for listed companies, rating agency downgrades to D-rating (which are public, dated, and tied to actual missed payments), or SEBI-mandated default disclosures under their regulations. The challenge is ensuring that if the signal set includes filings, the clock derived from rating actions doesn't create circularity—rating agencies might themselves be using the same governance flags, which would invalidate the whole test.

With fewer than 10 cases, you can't estimate lead-time distributions or AUC with confidence, but you can run a mechanism audit: reconstruct the timeline for each case and verify that flags preceded the default clock. The real fix, though, is reframing the target entirely. The customer cares about default, not IBC admission—admission is just the legal aftermath. So predicting the more frequent intermediate event (first default disclosure or D-rating) among listed companies solves both the sample size problem and the clock problem simultaneously. The universe of listed firms hitting D-rating in a given period is far larger than NCLT admissions, probably dozens per year. For the baseline, the fair comparison isn't vague "reading financials" but something like an Altman Z-score or similar established model. The false positive rate from governance flags firing frequently in small-caps is the real commercial problem — auditor resignations happen for benign reasons in Indian small-caps, so with tiny sample sizes they can't estimate how many are noise. I'd redesign this by anchoring the outcome to actual default events (SEBI LODR disclosures, missed debt payments, or rating downgrades to D) rather than admission dates, since admission lags default by years and makes any flags in that window look like echoes. The stronger approach would be prospective: freeze flag definitions now, score the universe monthly, then wait 6-12 months to count defaults among listed firms — with 30-60 defaults per year, a year of forward-looking data beats any retrospective study on a handful of cases. For the retrospective piece, I'd treat it as a pilot only — timeline case series on the clean cases anchored to actual default dates with matched controls, reported descriptively without significance testing or threshold optimization.
